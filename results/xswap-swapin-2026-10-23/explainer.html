Can prefetch make xswap reads faster?

Two ways to read pages before demand — their motivation, implementation and measured cost

The problem: each missing page can stop the application1. Read disk-backed pages ahead2. Decompress nearby pages aheadWhat we learned — and what to try next

The problem: each missing page can stop the application

When a needed page is outside RAM, the application faults and waits for it to return. Xswap gives that page a virtual swap slot: its contents may be compressed in RAM or written back to disk.

Original path in this 4 KiB test: fetch on demand

The baseline routes xswap through synchronous demand reads and skips ordinary readahead. That is reasonable for cheap in-memory reads, but written-back pages now require disk I/O. Sequential access may expose avoidable waiting.

Two different costs motivated two independent changes

ExperimentWhy try it?What actually changed
Disk-backed pagesUpcoming sequential accesses are predictable. Fetching neighbors could hide disk waiting.Check physical backing; route those faults through existing readahead. Speculative xswap neighbors must also have physical backing.
Compressed pagesNeighbors could enter the swap cache early, potentially reducing repeated demand-fault overhead.Check actual zswap residency; prefetch compressed neighbors into the swap cache. Each page is still decompressed synchronously.

Changed path: one fault also prepares likely next pages

Both patches start from the same descriptor-lifetime repair; they are not stacked. The goal is lower application read latency. The tradeoff is extra work on the current fault, especially when neighbors are not needed.
